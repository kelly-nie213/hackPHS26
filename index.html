A cost-benefit model for skyscrapers

Who gets the sunlight?

Shadow Ledger weighs what a tall building costs the public against what it gives back. The cost is the sunlight it blocks on streets, sidewalks and parks. The benefit is the homes inside it. We measure both for every building in eight U.S. downtowns.

The problem

Tall buildings are a trade-off

Benefit: new homes created

Cities often let developers build taller skyscrapers if they add something the public needs, usually housing.

Cost: lost sunlight

A tall tower also blocks sunlight on the streets and parks around it, and that hurts the climate too: shaded trees and plants grow less and absorb less carbon, and nearby rooftops can produce less solar power.

So... when a tower blocks sunlight, how many homes did the city get for it?

Application

Who would use it, and how

City planners

How: Check how existing towers score before approving new ones of a similar size.

Why: To make sure height bonuses actually get the city enough housing.

Climate and park advocates

How: Use the park rankings and heatmap to find the green spaces losing the most sun.

Why: To protect the trees and plants that cool cities and absorb carbon.

Developers and architects

How: Compare grades of buildings with different shapes and numbers of units.

Why: To design towers that add more homes for the shade they cast.

Homebuyers and renters

How: Go through the year to see how much sun a street or building gets.

Why: To know if an apartment or nearby park will be sunny or in shadow.

How it works

How the model works

We measure the cost (shade) and the benefit (homes) for each building, then divide one by the other.

  1. Replay the weather

    Using historical weather data, we find every hour in 2025 when the sun was actually out, and where it was in the sky.

  2. Measure the cost

    For each of those hours, we work out every building's shadow and how much public ground it covers: streets, sidewalks, plazas and parks. Rooftops don't count.

  3. ACF

    Compare it to the benefit

    We divide a building's average shade by its number of homes to get shade per home. Lower is better. Each building gets an A–F grade compared with the others in its city.

What's new

How this compares to a normal shadow study

Cities already require shadow studies for some new buildings. Those usually model one proposed tower on a few days of the year. We wanted to look at every building that already exists, over a full year, and weigh the shade against the housing.

Under the hood

How we built it

  • Data pipelineA Python script downloads buildings, home counts, parks and a year of hourly weather for each city.
  • Our own shadow mathWe wrote the sun-position and shadow code ourselves. It runs in background threads so the 3D map stays smooth.
  • 3D in the browserthree.js draws about 57,000 buildings at their real heights, with live shadows.
  • No build stepAbout 2,900 lines of plain JavaScript and Python. It runs from one command.

Data, limits & next steps

Sources

  • New York: NYC Open Data building footprints (surveyed roof heights) and PLUTO tax lots (actual homes per lot). Parks from NYC Parks.
  • Other cities: OpenStreetMap buildings, tower parts and parks. Heights come from mapped heights or floor counts.
  • Weather: Open-Meteo historical archive, with hourly sunshine duration, cloud cover and radiation.

Keep in mind

  • Outside New York, home counts are estimated from building type and floor area unless OpenStreetMap lists the number of units. Mixed-use towers may show no homes.
  • Buildings with no mapped height are drawn at a typical low-rise height.
  • Only towers inside each study area are counted, so parks at the edges are undercounted.
  • Where shadows overlap, both towers are charged.

What's next

  • Score proposed towers before they're approved, so the trade-off is known up front.
  • Printable report cards that community boards can bring to public hearings.
